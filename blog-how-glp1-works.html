What Is GLP-1?

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1 — a hormone produced naturally in the L-cells of your small intestine. It is released in response to food, and its job is to coordinate your body's metabolic response to a meal. It tells your pancreas to release insulin, signals your brain that you are full, slows the rate at which your stomach empties, and suppresses the release of glucagon — a hormone that raises blood sugar.

In people with obesity or type 2 diabetes, this system is often dysregulated. The GLP-1 signal is blunted, delayed, or insufficient. The appetite brake is weaker. The insulin response is less coordinated. The result is a body that struggles to self-regulate caloric intake and blood sugar — not because of a lack of willpower, but because of a measurable hormonal deficit.

GLP-1 receptor agonists are molecules designed to mimic and amplify this signal. They bind to the same receptors your natural GLP-1 binds to — but they stay active far longer than the hormone your body produces, which is degraded within minutes. The result is a sustained metabolic correction that changes how hunger, fullness, and energy metabolism function at a physiological level.

The Four Mechanisms That Drive Weight Loss

GLP-1 receptor agonists do not work through a single pathway. They act on multiple systems simultaneously — which is part of why their outcomes are so different from previous weight loss approaches.

Appetite suppression via the hypothalamus. GLP-1 receptors exist throughout the brain, including in the hypothalamus — the region that regulates hunger and satiety. When these receptors are activated, the brain receives a sustained "full" signal that reduces food-seeking behavior and decreases overall caloric intake. Patients consistently report that food simply becomes less compelling. Cravings diminish. Portion sizes naturally decrease without conscious restriction.

Slowed gastric emptying. GLP-1 receptor agonists delay the rate at which your stomach empties food into the small intestine. This extends the sensation of fullness after eating and reduces post-meal blood sugar spikes. Food stays with you longer — which means you feel satisfied on less.

Insulin secretion and blood sugar regulation. GLP-1 stimulates insulin release from the pancreas in a glucose-dependent manner — meaning it only amplifies insulin production when blood sugar is elevated. This makes it inherently safer than many older diabetes medications, which could cause blood sugar to drop too low regardless of what you ate.

Glucagon suppression. Glucagon is the hormone that signals the liver to release stored glucose into the bloodstream. GLP-1 receptor agonists suppress glucagon secretion, keeping blood sugar more stable between meals and reducing the metabolic signal that drives fat storage.

Why the Results Are Different

Every major weight loss intervention before GLP-1 therapy worked by asking the patient to do something — eat less, move more, restrict categories of food. The behavioral burden was placed entirely on the individual, fighting against hormonal and neurological systems that were actively working against them.

GLP-1 therapy changes the underlying physiology. Patients are not white-knuckling through hunger — they are operating from a neurological state in which hunger is genuinely reduced. This is why clinical trial participants do not plateau at 5-8% weight loss as they do with behavioral interventions alone. The brain's response to the drug changes the baseline, not just the behavior.

This also explains why the weight often returns when therapy is discontinued. The drug is not a cure — it is a correction. When the correction is removed, the underlying dysregulation returns. This is not a failure of the patient. It is the biology behaving exactly as expected.

How Different GLP-1 Drugs Compare

Not all GLP-1 receptor agonists are identical. The original drugs in this class — exenatide and liraglutide — required daily injections and produced modest weight loss of 5-8%. Semaglutide, administered weekly, represented a meaningful step forward with average weight loss of 15% in the STEP trials. Tirzepatide added GIP receptor agonism and pushed outcomes to approximately 20%. Retatrutide, in Phase 3 trials, has demonstrated up to 24% in Phase 2 data.

The trajectory is consistent: each generation has targeted more metabolic receptors with greater precision, and each has delivered meaningfully better outcomes.
